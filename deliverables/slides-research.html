Abstract  ·  149 words

Predicting building construction era from 30 m Landsat imagery is hard, and harder across cities: a model trained on Madrid must work in Amsterdam, where building materials, climate and urban form all differ. We compress each pixel’s 40-year, six-band reflectance series into per-pixel temporal statistics and train a class-balanced Random Forest on Madrid. Transfer rests on unsupervised, label-free alignment: class-conditional CORAL reshapes Madrid to Amsterdam one age class at a time, driven by the model’s own pseudo-labels (zero-shot macro-F1 0.36→0.65). Budget-scaled ZCA whitening with a small local classifier, blended with the Stage-1 model and smoothed over map-neighbours, handles the few-shot regime. By 100 labelled Amsterdam pixels per class the transferred model reaches Madrid’s own in-city score (0.72 vs 0.66). Residual error concentrates on pre-1984 classes, which carry no construction event in the record. Simple distribution alignment beat a learned embedding, ordinal loss, self-training and gradient boosting.

§1  The problem2 / 7

A new-city model is mis-tuned, not incapable

  • Sort each 30 m satellite patch into one of four age groups; score = macro-F1, all groups equal.
  • Train on Madrid; adapt to Amsterdam with 5–200 examples per group.
  • Two shifts: the buildings look different (brick vs concrete, wetter, older satellites) and the mix of ages differs (oldest group 1.6× more common).
Contributions

§2  The method3 / 7

Line the cities up first — the model does the rest

  • 40 years × 6 colours → ~100 numbers per patch. One row per patch, so the model must use the trend.
  • Key numbers: when the biggest change happened — the construction event, which looks the same in any city.
  • Predict Amsterdam once → reshape each Madrid age group to match → retrain. Twice. No answers used.
What we never touch
  1. Every line-up statistic comes from unlabelled Amsterdam; only the handful of given examples reach the model.

§3  A few local examples4 / 7

Few examples borrow from Madrid; many trust the local data

  • 5–25 examples → mostly trust the aligned Madrid model; the local one only nudges it.
  • 200 examples → mostly trust the local model.
  • The balance shifts on its own as the number grows — nothing hand-set per case.
  • We also “untangle” the features more gently when data is thin — that step needs enough examples to be stable.
The low-data case is a different recipe, not the full pipeline with less data.

§5  What we learned6 / 7

Simple alignment beat every clever alternative

Worked (score gain)

  • Line up the cities +0.07 no labels
  • Gentler untangle at low data +0.15 @ 5
  • Blend two models +0.04 @ 5
  • Neighbour smoothing +0.01 all

Didn’t — and why

  • Neural net − data already easy to separate
  • Ordinal training 0 smaller errors, not fewer
  • Fixing the age mix − confidence not trustworthy across cities
  • Self-training − teaches itself its own mistakes
  • Gradient boosting − collapses at 5 examples

Won’t claim

  • Two oldest groups can’t be split — both pre-1984, no construction event. A data limit; the literature agrees.
  • ~80% of our examples sit next to a test patch — we say so; smoothing stays prediction-only.
Every attempt to out-think the data lost to using it more carefully.
§6  Conclusion7 / 7

Cross-city transfer is a calibration problem

  1. A model in a new city is mis-tuned, not incapable — the line-up recovers most of the gap with zero labels.
  2. ~50 examples per group buy home-city accuracy; the low-data case needs its own recipe.
  3. On 30 m Landsat, careful use of the data beat every fancier model we tried.
What’s new here
  1. Lining the cities up group by group from the model’s own guesses, and the framing that few vs many examples need different machinery.
